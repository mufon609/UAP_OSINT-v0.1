## Description

Quantum Tomography of Negative Energy States in the Vacuum. This Defense Intelligence Reference Document in the Defense Futures series is one of a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2010 under the Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]), Advanced Aerospace Weapons System Applications (AAWSA) Program ([`/organizations/aawsap`]); the Prepared by and Author are (b)(3):10 USC 424 and (b)(6). The author ([`/people/eric-davis`]) is identified on another Defense Intelligence Agency document ([`/documents/dia-aatip-products-list-2018`]). The document is made available through the declassification research of John Greenewald, Jr. ([`/people/john-greenewald`]), creator of The Black Vault ([`/organizations/the-black-vault`]).

The Introduction introduces future aerospace vehicles that could have an advanced propulsion system using negative quantum vacuum energy to modify the spacetime geometry in the immediate vicinity surrounding the vehicle, to induce faster-than-light motion via traversable wormholes or warp drives, or even levitation via antigravity — exotic propulsion concepts the report describes as well-known in mainstream general relativity and quantum field theory research. While a physical state with negative energy is not familiar in classical physics, it is not rare in quantum field theory to have quantum states with negative energy density or a negative energy flux; even for a quantum scalar field in flat Minkowski spacetime the existence of such states is inevitable. There are two key examples of specially prepared quantum vacuum states known to produce small amounts of negative energy density in the laboratory — the well-known Casimir effect (a static quantum vacuum effect) and the squeezed vacuum states of the electromagnetic field (a time-domain quantum vacuum effect). The motivation is that, although we already make small amounts of negative energy in the laboratory via these two effects, we do not yet know whether we can access larger amounts for extended periods over extended spatial distributions; to measure and spatially map negative energy is the necessary first step before any study on producing large quantities of it. The report is based on a first step already taken by Hansen et al. (2001) for the time-domain negative energy pulses in squeezed electromagnetic vacuum states, and on Piotr Marecki's ([`/people/p-marecki`]) generalized analysis of the output of balanced homodyne detectors (BHDs) for the case of static negative energy states inside Casimir cavities.

The review section defines exotic matter (general relativity matter that possesses renormalized negative energy density, sometimes negative stress-tension or outward pressure — gravitational repulsion or antigravity) and the WEC, NEC, DEC, and SEC energy conditions, with the claim that all of the energy condition hypotheses have been experimentally tested in the laboratory and experimentally shown to be false 25 years before their formulation. It defines sub-vacuum energy equivalently as negative energy and reviews the origin of the quantum vacuum zero-point fluctuations, negative (sub-vacuum) energy in squeezed light — where one can in principle exploit quantum squeezing to extract energy from one place in the ordinary vacuum at the expense of accumulating excess energy elsewhere — and negative (sub-vacuum) energy in the Casimir effect, by far the easiest and most well known way to generate static negative energy in the lab. The Quantum Optical Homodyne Tomography section defines quantum tomography (from the Greek for slice, a method to infer the shape of a hidden object from its shadows or projections under various angles) and introduces Wigner functions, beam splitters, photodiodes, and balanced homodyne detection — amplifiers capable of detecting and quantifying vacuum and sub-vacuum fluctuations — as its measurement machinery, with figures drawn from Ulf Leonhardt ([`/people/ulf-leonhardt`]) and experimental data provided from the University of Tokyo ([`/organizations/university-of-tokyo`]).

The Balanced Homodyne Systems section introduces two experimental approaches. The time-domain system is based on the Hansen et al. pulsed BHD device, which reconstructed the Wigner function and density matrix of a coherent state with 99.5% fidelity at 91% quantum efficiency. The Casimir cavity (modified-Marecki) system is based on Marecki's proposed BHD that can detect and spatially map the sub-vacuum fluctuation region inside a Casimir cavity and measure its negative energy density spectrum — which uniquely addresses the Davies ([`/people/paul-davies`]) and Ottewill ([`/people/a-ottewill`]) negative energy detector hypothesis — together with Marecki's 2010 private communication claim that the Quantum Inequalities should also apply to static sub-vacuum fluctuations inside Casimir cavities. The CONCLUSION states the propulsion concept again. It goes on to recommend, first, that a research and development program be implemented to modify and commercialize a portable time-domain BHD device for detecting, measuring, and spatially mapping the negative energy regions produced by a putative pulsed (or "AC") negative energy generator; and, second, that an experimental program be implemented to test Marecki's modified BHD and the Quantum Inequalities theorem for Casimir geometries. In both cases the report notes that a number of such modified BHD devices could also be assembled in a sensor array for surveillance and detection of any anomalous aerospace platforms that might use engineered spacetime effects for propulsion. The ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS thank Professors Ulf Leonhardt and Piotr Marecki for contributing their lecture notes, references, and experimental data, and the document carries a References section.
