## Description

A Defense Intelligence Reference Document in the Defense Futures series — one of a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2010 under the Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]) Advanced Aerospace Weapons System Applications (AAWSA) Program ([`/organizations/aawsap`]). The paper reviews novel propulsion concepts utilizing plasmas (ionized gases) and magnetohydrodynamics (MHD): a review devoted to a group of emerging technologies centering on weakly ionized plasmas for propulsion and power, from electric (plasma) thrusters for spacecraft to air-breathing MHD engines, MHD energy bypass, and MHD applications to reentry and near-orbital flight.

On the space side, the document reviews electrothermal arcjet thrusters, electrostatic gridded ion thrusters, Hall-effect thrusters operating on xenon propellant, and magnetoplasmadynamic (MPD) thrusters, with photographs of thrusters being tested at the Jet Propulsion Laboratory ([`/organizations/jet-propulsion-laboratory`]), the University of Michigan ([`/organizations/university-of-michigan`]), and Princeton University ([`/organizations/princeton-university`]); it shows the NASA ([`/organizations/nasa`]) ion engine used on the Deep Space One spacecraft and a Lockheed Martin ([`/organizations/lockheed-martin`]) Series 7000 Comsat with Aerojet ([`/organizations/aerojet`]) 1.8-kW Arcjet thrusters for north-south station keeping. In its first-ever list of top 10 emerging aerospace technologies, released in 2009, the American Institute of Aeronautics and Astronautics (AIAA) ([`/organizations/aiaa`]) included two plasma technologies: plasma actuators for active flow control and plasma-based advanced space propulsion technologies.

For air-breathing flight, onboard generation and storage of electric power is identified as one of the main problems of high-altitude, high-speed flight: an MHD generator immediately downstream of a scramjet combustor can provide high power with no moving parts, and a conceptual high-speed air-breathing MHD engine is illustrated together with a photograph of the operating proof-of-concept experiment being investigated by Lockheed Martin Aeronautics. The principal problem for aeronautical MHD applications, however, is that normal air is not an electrical conductor: in "cold" air (at Mach <12), the complexity and power budget of nonequilibrium ionization and the weight and volume of a magnet make such flight devices quite impractical, unless a breakthrough in magnet and materials technologies occurs.

The Ajax, or Ayaks, concept of MHD energy bypass originated in the 1980s at Leninetz Scientific & Production Enterprise ([`/organizations/leninetz`]) in Leningrad, USSR, and has attracted perhaps the most attention over the last decade or two; a good overview of the concept is found, per the survey, in the Journal of Propulsion and Power Special Section devoted to weakly ionized gases for propulsion enhancement ([`/documents/jpp-special-section-weakly-ionized-plasmas-2008`]), with one of the authors of the survey serving as special guest editor. In criticizing the Ajax power cycle, D. Riggins ([`/people/d-riggins`]) — whose analysis ([`/documents/riggins-mhd-energy-bypass-jpp-2004`]) pointed out that as a propulsion power cycle the MHD bypass runs in the direction opposite to that dictated by thermodynamics — makes, per the survey, a significant mistake: in his derivations he assumes heat addition in the combustor occurs at stagnation rather than static temperature, in direct contradiction to the very idea of a scramjet. Against the calculations of the Ajax group in the paper by A. Kuranov ([`/people/a-kuranov`]) and A. Korabelnikov ([`/people/a-korabelnikov`]) ([`/documents/kuranov-korabelnikov-ajax-jpp-2008`]), the survey states with certainty that MHD energy bypass at Mach<12, where nonequilibrium ionization of air is required, is not a meaningful technology. At Mach>12, modeling by the group at NASA Ames ([`/organizations/nasa-ames-research-center`]) shows MHD bypass can indeed increase the Isp — though air-breathing flight at Mach>12 is not realistic given current materials, structures, and fuel development.

In contrast, a new reverse energy bypass with Virtual Cowl — a concept proposed by one of the authors of the survey together with his colleagues — is potentially practical for air-breathing hypersonic vehicles: analysis of the combined system with virtual cowl shows that thrust can actually increase by as much as 20-30%. For reentry and near-orbital flight, modeling shows that MW-scale power can be generated by a surface-integrated MHD system from 1 square meter of vehicle surface, and plasma and MHD technologies hold substantial and realistic promise to achieve hypersonic L/D of 3-10 — a game-changer that would enable aerodynamically assisted, on-demand orbital inclination changes.

The outlook for MHD propulsion could increase dramatically if hypersonic vehicles begin to carry powerful onboard electricity sources such as nuclear (fission or fusion) reactors — deployment of onboard nuclear power being, per the survey, mostly a political rather than a technological issue. Nuclear space reactors have been flown in space (SNAP-10A by the United States and TOPAZ reactors by the former Soviet Union), and a nuclear vehicle in orbit enables the beaming of power to air or ground vehicles or the use of laser and microwave weapons. The applications are identified as promising in the near future and as feasible and desirable for national defense — with nations such as Russia, China, and Japan likely to develop such technologies within several years and perhaps to be deploying them within 10 years.

### The core power-generation case for MHD on hypersonic vehicles: tens of kW to several MW with no moving parts from a generator placed downstream of a scramjet combustor.

> Onboard generation and storage of electric power is one of the main problems encountered with respect to high-altitude, high-speed flight. Hypersonic vehicles, both air-breathing and unpowered reentry "gliders," have no rotating turbomachinery to which an electrical generator could be connected. An attractive power option can be offered by magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) devices. For example, placing an MHD generator immediately downstream of a scramjet combustor can, given the high velocities and temperature of the flow and with metallic additives to the fuel, provide high power (from tens of kW to several MW) with no moving parts.

### The quantified cold-air verdict: even with the most efficient ionization, nonequilibrium MHD flight devices are impractical absent a breakthrough in ultralightweight ~10-Tesla magnets.

> Calculations show that the maximum ionization fraction is on the order of 10-6 and the maximum conductivity is on the order of 1 mho/m. With this low conductivity, substantial (S~0.1 or higher) MHD interaction parameters can only be reached with magnetic fields higher than several Tesla (i.e., 10-20 Tesla). The weight and volume of a magnet then makes such flight devices quite impractical, unless a breakthrough in magnet and materials technologies occurs resulting in ultralightweight magnets with B~10 Tesla.

### The survey's own technical correction of the published Riggins critique — Riggins' stagnation-temperature assumption contradicts the very idea of a scramjet, leaving MHD bypass a theoretical chance to increase Isp.

> However, in criticizing the Ajax power cycle and arguing that the Isp of Ajax is always less than that of a system without MHD bypass, D. Riggins26 makes a significant mistake. In his derivations, he assumes that combustion-generated heat addition in the combustor occurs at a gas temperature equal to the stagnation temperature of the flow (i.e., that the flow is fully stagnant in the combustor). This assumption is in direct contradiction to the very idea of a scramjet, where combustion occurs in supersonic flow. Heat addition in the combustor thus occurs at a static, not stagnation, temperature. It is this fact that at least gives MHD bypass a chance to increase Isp.
