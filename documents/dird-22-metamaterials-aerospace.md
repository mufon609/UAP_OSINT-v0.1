## Description

Metamaterials for Aerospace Applications. The Defense Intelligence Reference Document is 6 April 2010, with an ICOD of 1 December 2009 and control number DIA-08-1004-006, UNCLASSIFIED//FOR OFFICIAL USE ONLY, 28 pages. The Prepared by line is given as a component redacted under (b)(3):10 USC 424 and Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]); the Author line is redacted under (b)(6). In an Administrative Note the document is described as one in a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2009 under the Defense Intelligence Agency, Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program ([`/organizations/aawsap`]), with comments or questions to be addressed to the AAWSA Program Manager. The author ([`/people/g-shvets`]) is given as being from UT-Austin ([`/organizations/university-of-texas-austin`]) — the basis for which is a separate Defense Intelligence document ([`/documents/dia-aatip-products-list-2018`]); within the document the author's own research group at UT-Austin is given as having designed the first Plasmonic Negative Index Metamaterials (P-NIM) super-lens and the Wide-Angle "Perfect" Absorber of Mid-Infrared Radiation (WAPAMIR). On the document the Author line is redacted under (b)(6).


A metamaterial is defined as an artificial medium whose properties cannot be found in naturally-occurring materials, the study's emphasis being on electromagnetic and optical metamaterials. Such metamaterials can exhibit negative refractive index, which implies both negative electric permittivity and negative magnetic permeability (ε < 0, μ < 0) — the media once called "left-handed." A typical metamaterial consists of resonant elements such as Split Ring Resonators (SRR), whose sub-wavelength size distinguishes them from photonic crystals, and by properly designing magnetic SRRs any value of permeability μ can be achieved for a given frequency. The study reviews further concepts that have recently emerged — Indefinite Permittivity Metamaterials (IPM), Epsilon-Near-Zero (ENZ) metamaterials, and complementary metamaterials (CMMs) made of slits in a continuous metal screen. Its central aerospace point is miniaturization: it is this miniaturization that makes metamaterials interesting for aerospace application where small weight and size are essential, with the most exciting applications given as energy harvesting, non-reciprocal devices, enhancing the efficiency of nonlinear optical devices, imaging modalities capable of breaking the diffraction limit (super-lenses, hyper-lenses, far field super-lenses), and novel lithographic techniques.


On sub-diffraction imaging, the super-lens is given as one of the earliest applications of metamaterials, in which evanescent waves may be amplified so the image is transferred forward; one can envision space expeditions to other planets where it would be desirable to examine primitive cellular-level life in its natural environment. The hyper-lens uses a tapered indefinite permittivity (hyperbolic) medium, with one experimental UV implementation made of 16 layers of Ag/Al2O3, and the Far-Field Super-Lens (FSL) propagates sub-diffraction waves so they can be diffracted into the far field, with no need for a near-field scanning optical microscope. The author's group fabricated IPMs using SiO2-SiC-SiO2 multi-layers, investigating selectively-doped semiconductor multi-layers similar to those used by Gmachl ([`/people/claire-gmachl`])'s group at Princeton ([`/organizations/princeton-university`]), with the SiC films shipped by Professor Ferro ([`/people/ferro`]) from University of Lyons, France ([`/organizations/university-of-lyon`]). The sub-diffraction near-field plate of Merlin ([`/people/roberto-merlin`])'s invention (Reference 28) is given as the point from which it has become clear that interference between sub-diffraction electromagnetic fields can form a deeply sub-wavelength image.


On circuits and waveguide miniaturization, the speed of light sets a size limit (a structure processing a pulse of duration τ must be at least L = cτ long, so a 1 ns microwave pulse needs a device at least 1 ft long), which metamaterials circumvent by slowing down light: an axially varying heterostructure with a negative refractive index core can bring light to a complete standstill, with waveguide tapering forming a 'trapped rainbow' (Figure 15). Electromagnetically Induced Transparency (EIT) is shown using a plasmonic "molecule" coupling a radiative element with a subradiant (dark) element, and multiple layers of dark/bright antennas can achieve one of the most important manifestations of EIT, "slow" light. On energy harvesting, "perfect absorbers" of infrared radiation collect the Earth glow (maximum around λ>10 μm, most energy in 3 μm < λ < 14 μm) for night-time energy scavenging by high-altitude satellites; narrow-band absorbers can be used as bolometer arrays for space navigation off stellar infrared; an airborne platform could be powered by a high-power infrared laser source located on Earth; and a thermophotovoltaic (TPV) converter (for example GaSb, bandgap E_G = 0.7 eV near λ = 1.7 μm) is given for the advanced aerospace platforms of the future. The released document is cut off on its page 23 — satellites using the Earth glow for nighttime battery recharging, the collected power given as high, "...1 m² of black surface at" — without a References section, although the Contents page shows one.
