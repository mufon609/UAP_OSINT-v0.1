## Description

Cockpits in the Era of Breakthrough Flight. This Defense Intelligence Reference Document is one in a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2009 under the Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]) Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program ([`/organizations/aawsap`]), per its Administrative Notes; the 1 November 2010 cover, the ICOD 8 July 2010, DIA-08-1011-002, and the UNCLASSIFIED//FOR OFFICIAL USE ONLY on the document are set out in the structured fields below, as is the FY 2009 year the Administrative Notes give against the 1 November 2010 cover. The document was prepared by the Defense Intelligence Agency under (b)(3):10 USC 424, with an Author given only as (b)(6). The document was made available through The Black Vault declassification efforts of John Greenewald, Jr. The author off the document — on a separate Defense Intelligence reference document ([`/documents/dia-aatip-products-list-2018`]), Dr. G. Millis ([`/people/marc-millis`]), [`/organizations/tau-zero`] — is set out in the author note below.


Responding to the request to explore forefront science relevant to future cockpits for any form of aerospace craft and/or deep-space craft propelled by any unspecified advanced or breakthrough propulsion physics, the report offers a provisional cockpit design that employs predictions of propulsion physics breakthroughs, lessons of human-machine interface, and emerging technology for displays and controls. This study discusses the implications of breakthrough propulsion, including the mastery over gravitational and inertial forces and the prospect for faster-than-light spaceflight; the main reference used to predict these possibilities is the book Frontiers of Propulsion Science [Millis & Davis, 2009] (eds. Marc Millis ([`/people/marc-millis`]) and Eric Davis ([`/people/eric-davis`])). The author states these interpretive predictions and cockpit implications are solely generated by the author and have not yet been published or debated with other scientists and engineers, and should be considered an initial step into the process. The Introduction notes that all projections in this report are based on public domain information.


Breakthroughs in propulsion physics — control over gravitational or inertial forces, propellant-less space drives, and even faster-than-light travel — are not imminent, but enough progress has been made to allow for thoughtful speculation about their nature and implications. For the sake of bracketing the scope of coverage, the study assumes practical star flight will be limited to a 100-light-year radius around the Sun. The most significant and perplexing difference for breakthrough-era cockpits is that the sensations of motion inside the vehicle will not necessarily match the motion of the vehicle itself — the necessary distinction between external and internal force environments that the report sets out as a double-hull vehicle model, illustrated by the example of the Alcubierre warp drive, which uses the Riemannian geometry of Einstein's general relativity to create a "warp bubble" around the craft. Among the technical proposals is using the cosmic microwave background as a reference frame against which velocity can be measured relative to the mean rest frame of the universe (a "universal speedometer"). One consequence of the double-hull configuration is the loss of direct outside visual contact — "In other words, do not expect windows" — and a turn to a virtual surround display.


The human-machine interface chapter applies human-factors lessons, with one exception made when considering emerging technologies: brain implants for BMIs and reengineering humans (transhumanism) are not considered, the cockpit instead accommodating the norms and limitations of human perception. A brain-machine interface for issuing commands with thoughts is assumed fully matured but constrained to non-invasive techniques (avoiding the need to modify the pilots) — citing at Endnote 19 the Defense Intelligence Reference Document on controlling external devices in the absence of limb-operated interfaces ([`/documents/dird-12-bci-controlling-external-devices`]). As a provisional baseline the report chooses a pair of six-degree-of-freedom joysticks at the edge of the cockpit chair's arm rests for the full six degrees of freedom of a breakthrough vehicle. The Future Work chapter adds a caveat that advances in cockpits for breakthrough flight might be further advanced by taking advantage of the gaming industry or through science fiction speculation. The document is followed by an Annotated Bibliography (Appendix A) and an Endnotes section of 52 endnotes (Appendix B).
