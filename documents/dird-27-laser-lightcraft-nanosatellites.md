## Description

Laser Lightcraft Nanosatellites. This Defense Intelligence Reference Document is one in a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2010 under the Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]) Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program ([`/organizations/aawsap`]), per its Administrative Notes; the 1 November 2010 cover, the ICOD 30 August 2010, DIA-08-1011-001, and the UNCLASSIFIED//FOR OFFICIAL USE ONLY marked on the document are given in the Document Summary. The Prepared by line gives the Defense Intelligence Agency with the (b)(3):10 USC 424 component, and the Author line gives only (b)(6). The document was made available through The Black Vault declassification efforts of John Greenewald, Jr. The author given off the document — on a separate Defense Intelligence reference document ([`/documents/dia-aatip-products-list-2018`]), Dr. E. Davis ([`/people/eric-davis`]) — is given in the author note below.


Laser propulsion is presented as a new and exceptional method for reaching space: by launching spacecraft on a beam of electromagnetic radiation, it would be the first new method of achieving orbit since the late 1950's. The laser-propelled vehicle, called "Lightcraft" because it flies on a beam of laser light, is designed to harness the energy of a laser beam and convert it into propulsive thrust. A more recent incarnation of this concept, developed by the Air Force Research Laboratory ([`/organizations/afrl`]) at Edwards AFB, has the Lightcraft operate in two propulsion modes — airbreathing (detonation wave) and rocket ablation (deflagration). The laser Lightcraft project originally grew out of the Lightcraft Technology Demonstration Program funded by the Strategic Defense Initiative Organization ([`/organizations/sdio`]) Laser Propulsion Program in the late 1980's; in the 1990's a joint program involving the NASA-Marshall Space Flight Center ([`/organizations/nasa`]) and the Propulsion Sciences and Advanced Concepts Division of the AFRL Propulsion Directorate developed and tested an experiment whose successful tests at the White Sands Missile Range High Energy Laser Systems Test Facility demonstrated the first passively controlled vertical free flight of an object propelled by the U.S. Army's 10 kW Pulsed Laser Vulnerability Test System infrared CO2 laser. The laser propulsion work is in collaboration with Leik Myrabo's ([`/people/leik-myrabo`]) laser propulsion group at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute ([`/organizations/rensselaer-polytechnic-institute`]).


On cost, the system would reduce space launch costs by two to three orders of magnitude below today's levels, with estimated launch costs of $20/kg to $600/kg of payload. Preliminary life-cycle cost estimates made during the AFRL study by Froning ([`/people/hugh-froning`]) and Davis (reference [26], Froning, H. D., and Davis, E. W., 2006) indicated that transportation system costs for placing 10 kg, 5.0 kg, and 1.0 kg of mass into orbit using Lightcraft and ground-based lasers would be approximately 3, 5, and 15 times less than with conventional rockets. The Conclusion gives the most promising military mission as the placement of Earth and space observing nano-/pico-satellites of up to 3 kg mass into LEO, with other potential missions including ground/sea- or airborne-launched kinetic kill weapons to shoot down enemy ballistic missiles. Launching such satellites requires megawatt-class lasers; the report gives TEXTRON Systems Corporation's ([`/organizations/textron-systems`]) proposed 10 MW electron gun-driven CO₂/gas mixture laser as a multi-megawatt-class system that can be implemented now, while the emerging Bulk Slab Solid-State, high-power Fiber, and Free-Electron laser technologies — Northrop Grumman's ([`/organizations/northrop-grumman`]) Joint High Power Solid-State Laser and the DARPA ([`/organizations/darpa`]) High Energy Liquid Laser Area Defense System among them — offer lower system and infrastructure cost. The Conclusion states, "This author recommends that the Department of Defense, in collaboration with NASA, return laser Lightcraft propulsion R&D to the United States" and restart the space launch test flight demonstration program of the Air Force X-50LR Lightcraft, which was originally proposed by Dr. Frank Mead ([`/people/franklin-mead`]). The document is followed by a References section of 35 references.
