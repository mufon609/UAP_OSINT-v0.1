## Description

Quantum Computing and Utilizing Organic Molecules in Automation Technology. This Defense Intelligence Reference Document in the Defense Futures series, 10 December 2010, with an ICOD of 10 December 2010 and document number DIA-08-1102-005, UNCLASSIFIED//FOR OFFICIAL USE ONLY, is one in a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2010 under the Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]) Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program ([`/organizations/aawsap`]). The Prepared by and Author lines read (b)(3):10 USC 424 and (b)(6); the author is identified on another Defense Intelligence Agency document ([`/documents/dia-aatip-products-list-2018`]). This document is made available through the declassification efforts and research of John Greenewald, Jr. ([`/people/john-greenewald`]), creator of The Black Vault ([`/organizations/the-black-vault`]).

Powerful onboard computing hardware is a desired option for future space travel: without large data processing capability, the copious amounts of data acquired during flight from astronomical sensors as well as crew and vehicle sensors will need to be sent back to earthbound machines for processing, introducing delays measured in hours for routine calculations, and current commercial computer hardware trajectories in silicon substrate semiconductors are not likely to produce a radiation-hard or small and portable supercomputer without significant mission-specific alteration. Against that motivator the treatise concentrates on alternatives to traditional computing technology — computers based on entangled quantum states and molecular computing hardware based on DNA molecules — through new architectures (logic gates and a general Turing machine) explored in the context of quantum computing with an emphasis on organic molecules as a core technology. The review includes a significant introduction to the necessary elements of quantum computing and a summary of the state-of-the-art technologies, then background on DNA and production of engineered DNA chains, DNA logic gates, and nanomachines that will repair DNA circuitry, with forecasts at the 10-20 and 40-year horizons.

The quantum material moves through the closed box and fault tolerance requirement (decoherence, the destructive process by which any external influence disturbs the state of a quantum system, requires circuits to be isolated from the rest of the universe), the quantum dot approach built on Pauli's two-state electron spin, electrostatic quantum dots in graphene, and a summary of additional inorganic technologies (ion and atomic traps). It then turns to DNA-based designs for molecular computers — DNA as the most logical solution that resides within our own bodies when silicon-based integrated circuit architectures reach their restriction — across DNA background, self-assembly, DNA origami, engineering DNA-based logic gates, deoxyribozyme-based Boolean automata, the DNA robot, and DNA nanomotors, with cryptography (cracking cipher codes) the driving application behind quantum computing. The forecasts hold that the first operating quantum computers capable of solving real-world problems will commence within 10 years and be based on ion trap technology but are not practical for space travel due to cryogenic and EM shielding needs; that hybrid designs utilizing arrays of quantum dots and photon communication channels will be an option for space travel supercomputing on the 40-year timescale, with spintronics likely an essential ingredient; and that simple organic computing based on DNA tiles will be realized in the next 20 years, with useful DNA-based devices as essential space exploration tools at 40 years, their advantage over solid state devices being the ability to repair nanoscale elements damaged in normal use or by cosmic radiation.

The Conclusion sets out four major advances in space-ready technology to be seen on the 40-year horizon: devices developed for optical computing allowing an all-optical computer to augment or replace general purpose computers; optical communication playing an essential role in hybrid designs of space-based quantum computers along with quantum dots; DNA devices performing simple distributed sensor data analyses; and fault tolerant DNA computers available for mission-critical analysis tasks. The body reports results from outside research groups — among them a NASA space test of the technology cancelled in late 2009, single electron spin qubit research, the autonomous molecular robot made out of DNA, light-powered and cargo carrying DNA nanomotors, and the MAYA deoxyribozyme automata that play a game of tic-tac-toe against a human player — and a numbered References section of 130 entries.

### Defines decoherence and the closed-box isolation requirement — a load-bearing constraint on all quantum-computing hardware in the report.

> A fundamental principle of quantum mechanics is that any external influence on a system necessarily disturbs the state of that system. This influence could be external disruption or internal leakage − either interaction will change the internal quantum state. This destructive process is known as decoherence. External influences will disturb the system and thus the circuits need to be isolated from the rest of the universe, also known as the 'closed box' requirement.
