## Description

A Defense Intelligence Reference Document produced under the Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]) Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program [`/organizations/aawsap`], one in a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2009. The Administrative Note states a COPYRIGHT WARNING that further dissemination of the photographs in this publication is not authorized. The Author line shows only [(b)(6)] and the Prepared by line shows the Defense Intelligence Agency, so the document shows no author; it is one of the FY 2009 AAWSA Program advanced technology reports ([`/documents/dia-aatip-products-list-2018`]). The document is made available through the declassification efforts and research of John Greenewald, Jr. ([`/people/john-greenewald`]), creator of The Black Vault ([`/organizations/the-black-vault`]).

A survey of technological approaches to controlling external devices in the absence of limb-operated interfaces. The technology endpoint the paper seeks is thought-based operation of remote machinery during normal human activities without mechanical device interaction — without the need to go to a shielded room, to remain perfectly motionless to reduce signal noise, or to interact with a normal electro-mechanical device. Data transfer rates are sought above 5-10 bits/second (referred to as high-bandwidth BMIs), with a 1-of-N command response targeted at 300 milliseconds or less. The paper concludes that noninvasive electrical monitoring of neural activity, primarily reading combined action of muscles and neurons, is the most promising commercial technology in the near term, and argues mainly by eliminating other approaches that the most probable long-term technology involves invasive single-neuron-based direct cortical connections to form a network of high-bandwidth duplex communication pathways.

The discussion of signal sources states that BMI relies on the electrical activity of neurons: all technologies directly measuring human neuronal action rely on detecting or influencing electrical activity, and no current in situ research selectively affects neurotransmitter activity for information exchange. The discussion includes all-or-nothing action potential transmission down the axon, the P300 signal as a prime candidate for a trainable interface, and the indirect Blood Oxygen Level Dependent (BOLD) effect monitored by Magnetic Resonance Imaging or Near Infrared Spectroscopy, whose 4-6 second response peak limits its useful applications. BMI design is divided into open-loop and closed-loop systems; the bulk of research in direct neural interfaces is toward restoring mechanical capabilities to those who have lost limbs or limb control, whereas for a healthy individual a hand movement to control a mechanical device is far more practical.

There are four noninvasive technologies in widespread use for monitoring brain activity — electroencephalography (EEG) and magnetoencephalography (MEG) as direct measures, functional MRI (fMRI) and near-infrared spectroscopy (NIRS) as indirect BOLD measures. The EEG discussion covers commercial dry-electrode devices for consumers and the doubt about whether they read neural signals at all rather than facial muscle movement. MEG is dependent on superconducting quantum interference devices (SQUIDs) sensitive to 100 femtoTesla changes, and 100 femtoTesla will prove an insurmountable barrier to sifting signal from noise outside a heavily-shielded, metal-free environment. The electromyogram (EMG) approach uses the muscles as a biological amplifier of neural signals, with 1-2 bits per second possible with minimal training. fMRI has unmatched 3D spatial resolution and complete skull penetration, but the several seconds BOLD delay makes it inappropriate for many naturalistic applications; and NIRS correlates with fMRI at reduced coverage and lower resolution.

The invasive technologies discussion presents the cochlear implant — the most prolific human invasive BMI, an open loop stimulation system through a peripheral connection — then the direct cortical array, penetrating cortical electrodes and their scar tissue response, MEMS/ECoG/PSoC chips that fuse biological to physical circuitry external to the body, the Fetz ([`/people/eberhard-fetz`]) autonomous battery operated neural implant, chronic neural implants with fMRI, new electrode designs that are MR compatible, and hybrid neuro-robotic systems including a lamprey brain stem closed loop pointing to cerebellar interface points. Trials using human subjects present the ethical dilemma of implanting normal volunteers and a 2002 median nerve self-experimenter trial whose connection lasted 3 months before it deteriorated beyond use; optical stimulation that uses light to gate action potentials through a locally inserted LED is presented as a path with no contact between the physical circuits and neurons, avoiding scar tissue. The Discussion states that any successful BMI relies on brain plasticity through user training, neural decoding by a machine learning algorithm, and neuroscience knowledge, and the Conclusions state that noninvasive electrical sensors that rely primarily on EMG signals will dominate near term real-world applications and surpass traditional electro-mechanical interfaces within 5 years, while the far term high-bandwidth (5-20 bits/second) path is an invasive approach using interfaces with single neurons in the cortex or cerebellum, with optical interfaces and ex-vivo electrode arrays encased in engineered neural tissue the two most promising research paths.

### Establishes the all-or-nothing action-potential physics and the 1-10 millisecond action-potential duration that set the sampling requirements for any direct-detection BMI

> The signal transmission down the axon of a neuron is an all-or-nothing process. When the cell body is stimulated above threshold, the axon transmits the same action potential at the same speed and in the same direction, regardless of the extent above threshold or duration of the input. Action potentials have durations of 1-10 milliseconds.

### Defines the P300 evoked signal — a pulse from a large group of neurons about 300 milliseconds after a stimulus, influenced by top-down executive function and therefore a prime candidate for a trainable interface

> P300 is a term used to indicate a pulse signal from a large group of neurons that appears about 300 milliseconds after a stimulus event. Peaks that occur between 200 and 400 milliseconds are commonly grouped into the P300 category. The "P" stands for a positive measured voltage, while "N" signals are negative.³ The P300 signal has been shown to be influenced by top-down executive function and is therefore a prime candidate for a trainable interface (Reference 5).

### Key MEMS/ECoG finding — fusing biological to physical circuitry external to the body makes the implant connection biological-to-biological, avoiding the scar-tissue degradation that plagues penetrating chronic probes; one of the two headline far-term paths

> One advantage of these devices is that the fusing of biological to physical circuitry is performed external to the body and the interface connection of the implants is thus biological to biological. This avoids a natural reaction of the nervous system to form scar tissue around penetrating chronic physical probes.¹⁸ Scar tissue leads to degradation of proximal signal transfer over time. These neural-tissue encapsulated chips offer significant advantages over other technologies because of their ability to be rapidly integrated into the biological environment.
