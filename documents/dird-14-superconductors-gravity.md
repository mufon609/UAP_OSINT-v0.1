## Description

The Role of Superconductors in Gravity Research is a historical survey of the role superconductors have played in the recent search for laboratory-scale manipulation of gravity. It is one in a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2009 under the Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]), Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program ([`/organizations/aawsap`]); the program's list of these reports ([`/documents/dia-aatip-products-list-2018`]) gives the author ([`/people/george-hathaway`], [`/organizations/hathaway-consulting`]), the document itself with no author given. References 9, 35, and 43 are works whose authorship is that of the same author. The reference date and the redacted author are given in the Document Summary above.
The Introduction is a survey of how the invention of superconductors, and in particular the recent development of high-temperature ceramic superconductors, provided the impetus for pursuing a connection between gravity, electromagnetism, and magnetism and matter in the solid state. The discovery of yttrium-barium-copper oxide (YBCO) ceramics able to superconduct at liquid nitrogen temperatures allowed laboratories to fabricate these superconductors in experimentally useful sizes; for the theoretician the possibility of considering the superconductor being a macroscopic quantum object a reality rather than a fantasy suggested avenues for developing theories connecting gravity and gravity-like forces to engineerable matter, and for the experimentalist extrapolations from these theories suggested there might be gravitational disturbances in the laboratory amenable to measurement.
In the Gravity Waves section a distinction is made between gravitational waves, the gravitational force, and anomalous forces, with gravitational wave work divided into low frequency and high frequency realms. Several researchers consider that high-frequency gravitational waves (HFGW) will be produced in the laboratory under certain conditions in the near future, with a considerably more ambitious view of the future than the low-frequency researchers, including the use of HFGW for communications, telescopy, microscopy, and possibly propulsion, possibly mediated or assisted by superconductors; current research on the link between HFGW and the manipulation of gravity for propulsion is at present only theoretical.
The Gravitoelectromagnetism section is a review of novel explanations of gravitational attraction, including Puthoff's ([`/people/hal-puthoff`]) development of an idea originally put forward by Sakharov ([`/people/andrei-sakharov`]) that posits gravity as a Casimir-like attraction arising within the universal sea of fluctuating electromagnetic interactions, the zero-point fluctuations. The attraction of modifying gravity for propulsive purposes lies in two categories of effect — modifying or negating the gravitational attraction of a nearby body, and providing propulsive force based on the same underlying phenomenon — and Braginski ([`/people/vladimir-braginski`]) et al. showed that ordinary matter cannot be used to generate measurable gravitational fields in the laboratory. It is possible to reformulate the tensor format of general relativity into a vector format valid in the weak field approximation, with the gravitomagnetic field and the gravitoelectric field as analogs of the magnetic and electrostatic fields of electromagnetic theory.
The Historical Timeline is the bulk of the document, following the modern search for a link between electromagnetism, matter, and gravity. Podkletnov ([`/people/evgeny-podkletnov`]) and Nieminen ([`/people/risto-nieminen`]) published what is considered the first possible evidence for an experimental link between high-temperature liquid nitrogen (LN2) superconductor effects and gravity, allegedly in the form of a gravity shield, but many scientists have cast considerable doubt on their findings; the experiment has remained controversial, and most damning to Podkletnov's case was the complete lack of any supporting evidence that the experiment had ever actually taken place. The theoretical work of Li ([`/people/ning-li`]) and Torr ([`/people/douglas-torr`]), which expanded on earlier work by DeWitt ([`/people/bryce-dewitt`]) and Ross ([`/people/dennis-k-ross`]), later saw the critique by Harris ([`/people/edward-harris`]), who argued that the correct estimation of the induced gravitoelectric field outside a superconductor is some 20 orders of magnitude smaller, with no rebuttal forthcoming from Li or Torr. The author's own version of the Podkletnov spinning disk experiment, completed in late 2001 and published in 2003, showed a null result and represented the closest published replication of the original. Tajmar ([`/people/martin-tajmar`]) and De Matos ([`/people/clovis-de-matos`]) claimed to have found the expected large gravitomagnetic field matched to within a factor of 1.5 of their theoretical results, set against the concerns Eric Davis ([`/people/eric-davis`]) raised that the basis for calculating the Cooper pair mass is so fraught with uncertainties as to leave Tajmar's mass anomaly unfounded; by 2007 Tajmar recognized that new data did not match their prior predictions while an unexplained residual signal persisted with a relatively large coupling constant between the observed acceleration effect and the applied angular velocity. As of the writing there had been no further pronouncements from Podkletnov, no published replication of his gravity beam experiment, and more and more scientists were coming to the conclusion that the experiment was never actually performed.
The Conclusion: although the payoff of the discovery of a superconductor-mediated interaction between matter and gravity would be tremendous, only a few researchers are pursuing this goal, the main reason being the adherence to dogma concerning the impossibility of increasing the matter/electromagnetic coupling coefficients, reinforced by reputable physicists pointing out that the theoretical constructs presented so far are based on questionable foundations, and that the likelihood of scientific ridicule is extremely high in the search for laboratory-scale gravitational interactions.
