## Description

Space Access: Where We've Been . . . and Where We Could Go. The Defense Intelligence Reference Document is 8 March 2010, with an ICOD of 1 December 2009 and control number DIA-08-1001-006, UNCLASSIFIED//FOR OFFICIAL USE ONLY. Where the Prepared by and Author appear, the document shows [(b)(3):10 USC 424] and Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]) and [(b)(6)]. An Administrative Note states the product is one in a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2009 under the Defense Intelligence Agency, [(b)(3):10 USC 424] Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program [`/organizations/aawsap`], and that comments or questions should be addressed to the AAWSA Program Manager. The document is one of a series of FY 2009 AAWSA Program advanced technology reports ([`/documents/dia-aatip-products-list-2018`]); the author ([`/people/paul-czysz`]) appears there, not on the document, where the Author line shows [(b)(6)].

The Introduction states that development of commercial access to space is a straightforward effort dominated by propulsion and reliability, with the initial focus on schedulable, dependable access to and from low Earth orbit (LEO). It states the means have been known for years but a dedicated organized effort has been lacked: the key requirement is to develop a robust infrastructure, and this is a matter of skill, operational hardware based on durable, reliable, and demonstrated components, and operational systems — not necessarily a matter of technology. The account presents October 1958, when the author's job in the vertical wind tunnel at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base abruptly changed to hypersonic and high-temperature flows, as what was then the Aircraft Laboratory became the Air Force Flight Dynamics Laboratory (AFFDL) and Al Draper ([`/people/alfred-draper`]) began working with a select group of aerospace firms on hypersonic gliders; a group of Aerospace Corporation veterans, asked about space access at the time, responded that it was not a technology issue, it was a hardware issue. With the orbiting of Sputnik, the aircraft path to space represented by the X series of planes ended with the X-15, replaced by the strategy of blasting to space with expendable rockets derived from ballistic missile hardware.

The body presents two basic configuration types for a transportation system to and from space: a hypersonic glider powered by rocket or air-breathing rocket cycle propulsion and derived from the FDL series of hypersonic gliders developed by the AFFDL and the work of the McDonnell Douglas Astronautics Company, and an air-breathing propulsion-configured vehicle where the underside of the vehicle is an integral part of the propulsion system. It documents the AFFDL's 1958-68 hypersonic glider configurations (the FDL-7C/D and Model 176 lifting bodies), their high lift-to-drag ratios, cross ranges in excess of 4,500 nautical miles permitted return with no waiting, and the weight factor debate between high-performance lifting bodies and ballistic capsules, citing a 1965 Stephens report. The Rocket Propulsion section compares the Pratt & Whitney XLR-129 — a shuttle-class engine brought to full pressure operation in just over three months — with the space shuttle main engine, which required three years and never demonstrated reusability without overhaul. The Up-and-Down Operations section states that an a priori dictate of horizontal takeoff has thwarted many launcher studies and given air-breathing propulsion a "too heavy" label since the first aerospace plane in 1958.

The Conclusion presents the question of where the space infrastructure is forty years after Apollo, comparing one way trips to LEO to the pioneers' Conestoga wagons, and states the space business has it backwards: there is no commerce until the infrastructure is in place, not vice versa, just as neither the United Kingdom nor the United States had long-distance, two-way commerce until the railroads were established. It states this is not a technology issue — that we have known for 50 years how to create a LEO infrastructure — and that a commitment as dedicated as the Apollo program will be necessary to build a space infrastructure around Earth and the moon.

### Glider capability — the FDL-7C class has hypersonic L/D ratios above 2.7, giving unpowered cross ranges in excess of 4,500 nautical miles so the craft can depart any low orbit and land in CONUS or continental Europe

> The hypersonic glider based on the FDL-7C and the hypersonic air-breathing aircraft in Figure 8 both have hypersonic L/D ratios in excess of 2.7. In very practical terms, that means unpowered cross ranges in excess of 4,500 nautical miles and down ranges on the order of the Earth's circumference. So these two craft can depart from any location of a low-altitude orbit and land in CONUS or in continental Europe.
