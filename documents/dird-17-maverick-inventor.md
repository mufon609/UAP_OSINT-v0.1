## Description

Maverick Inventor Versus Corporate Inventor: Where Will the Next Major Innovations Arise? is an analysis of where the next major innovations in energy and propulsion are most likely to arise — from the lone "maverick" inventor or from the "corporate" inventor who is a part of a large organization. It is one in a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2009 under the Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]), Advanced Aerospace Weapon System Applications (AAWSA) Program ([`/organizations/aawsap`]); on the program's list of these reports ([`/documents/dia-aatip-products-list-2018`]) the author is given as Hathaway ([`/people/george-hathaway`], [`/organizations/hathaway-consulting-services`]), the document itself carrying no author. Reference 10 and Reference 27 are works by that same author. It is related to [`/documents/dird-14-superconductors-gravity`], carrying no author given, the same AAWSA Administrative Note, and released by the same Black Vault FOIA clearinghouse.
The Introduction examines how, in the early 20th century, the solitary maverick inventor was responsible for the bedrock of most of today's industrial and commercial enterprises, but as those enterprises grew larger they could afford their own research and development departments where improvements to the current technologies were tightly managed rather than true innovation, and the university, the government think tank, and the military research and development department arose as additional institutions where true innovation is fostered. The paper concentrates on two areas where solutions have been particularly hard to come by — energy, and propulsion, in particular aerospace and space propulsion — and considers that new and exotic materials and the capital outlay required to probe the limits of current high technology in search of a breakthrough are the major reasons for the shift in focus from the lone maverick inventor to the corporate inventor.
The Maverick Inventor section characterizes the lone inventor as drawn to "low-hanging fruit" since the time of Michael Faraday ([`/people/michael-faraday`]), his creativity stimulate by the Internet and by movies that depict the lone genius, and increasingly given to intellectual and experimental laziness and a decreasing reliance on sound technical education. It uses a list of categories of maverick inventor (by formal training and money) and draw on the inventor Dean Kamen ([`/people/dean-kamen`]) and entrepreneur Guy Kawasaki ([`/people/guy-kawasaki`]) on the maverick personality. The Corporate Inventor section describes the inventor who is a part of a large organization conveniently labeled a "think tank" — government, military, academic, corporate, or privately funded — with access to information, technical apparatus, business acumen, and "blue-sky" teams — government laboratories sometimes allow such teams to investigate unusual areas, a particular example being Tajmar ([`/people/martin-tajmar`]) — set against the drawbacks of fluctuations in the overall economy, the Not Invented Here (NIH) syndrome, burnout, and "group think." Table 1 provides examples of the various types of think tanks, among them SRI International ([`/organizations/stanford-research-institute`]), NASA, DARPA, and the Perimeter Institute.
The Technologies section concentrates on unconventional energy and propulsion, and on the "new primary energy sources" the highlighted inventors investigate — permanent magnets, cold and warm fusion, "zero-point fluctuations," batteries and rotating systems, and approaches to modulating the local gravity field — and the maverick contributions of Tesla ([`/people/nikola-tesla`]) to electrical power generation and of Tsiolkovsky ([`/people/konstantin-tsiolkovsky`]) and Goddard ([`/people/robert-goddard`]) to the foundations of modern rocketry, set against the corporate inventors who now drive the efficiency innovations. The Type sections then examine the inventor categories from the Type 1 inventor (no formal training, little money) through the Type 5 inventor (a small group of inventors), considering each type's measurement, education, funding, and likelihood of contributing innovation, with Ovshinsky ([`/people/stanley-ovshinsky`]) included as a self-taught Type 4 genius and a 2003 NASA tutorial by Millis ([`/people/marc-millis`]) used for his division of "pioneers" and "masters."
The Comparisons section provides Table 2, which compares the essential elements that characterize the types of inventor — age, motivation, realism, bureaucracy, capitalization, education, business sense, measurement, lab skills, externals, flexibility, and media interest — used as criteria for determining the inventor type most likely to produce the necessary innovations in advanced primary energy and exotic propulsion. In the Conclusions, as far as maverick inventors are concerned, Types 3 and 4 appear to be the best choice for where the next innovations will arise; the corporate inventor, and especially the university-based inventor, if motivated and granted sufficient flexibility, would be an excellent candidate to carry out the required innovations and rival Types 3 and 4; Type 5 inventors are the next best possibility, with Types 1 and 2 merely being watched casually, as no substantial innovation is expected to arise from these quarters.
