## Description

One of a series of advanced technology reports produced in FY 2010 under the Defense Intelligence Agency ([`/organizations/dia`]), Advanced Aerospace Weapons System Applications (AAWSA) Program ([`/organizations/aawsap`]). The document covers ultracapacitors — energy storage devices capable of extremely rapid charge and discharge rates, with the ability to be cycled hundreds of thousands of times — for commercial and military applications, contrasting them with lithium ion batteries: less than 10% of the specific energy, but ideal where power is to be delivered or received quickly.
In 1966, Standard Oil Company of Ohio (SOHIO) ([`/organizations/sohio`]) invented the device in the format now commonly used; NEC ([`/organizations/nec`]) introduced the SuperCapacitor™ in 1978 under license from SOHIO, and in the 1980s Matsushita Electric Company ([`/organizations/matsushita-electric`]) developed a method of manufacturing ultracapacitors with improved electrodes. Application has expanded from electronics and telecommunications to industrial load leveling and transportation; earliest uses of ultracapacitors were in motor startup for tanks and submarines, and early adoption by the military led to cost reductions. Ultimately, application will be pulsed power storage and delivery for electric propulsion and directed-energy weapons. Chapter 3 addresses "Signatures and Vulnerabilities" and "Stimulated Discharge" — because ultracapacitors have very high discharge rates, they will emit a distinctive broadband RF signature when discharged, and vulnerabilities likely exist and warrant study.
Chapter 4 describes a vertically aligned CNT forest produced at Lockheed Martin's ([`/organizations/lockheed-martin`]) Advanced Technology Center (LMATC) and CNT-coated glass fibers from the NEARLab facility; graphene, where Lockheed Martin is actively engaged in research; the discoveries at Drexel University ([`/organizations/drexel-university`]) in carbide-derived carbons; and the magnetic capacitor (MCap) that Northern Lights Semiconductor Corp. (NLSC) ([`/organizations/northern-lights-semiconductor`]) has been developing since 2007 — at its mass production target, MCaps would disrupt both the lithium ion battery and the ultracapacitor market. Per the Conclusions, development of these advanced ultracaps "must be performed, understood, and adopted by the U.S. government" and we "must have a keen understanding and awareness of the unique spectral signatures these devices generate."
This document is made available through the declassification efforts and research of John Greenewald, Jr. ([`/people/john-greenewald`]), creator of The Black Vault ([`/organizations/the-black-vault`]), the largest online Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) document clearinghouse in the world.

### The document's thesis — what ultracapacitors are and their tradeoff against lithium-ion batteries

> Ultracapacitors (ultracaps) are energy storage devices capable of extremely rapid charge and discharge rates with the ability to be cycled hundreds of thousands of times. These unique capabilities make ultracaps attractive for a number of applications. The advantages over lithium ion batteries are somewhat mitigated by the fact that they have less than 10% of the specific energy (Wh/kg) and require a power converter to regulate their voltage.

### The document's distinctive intelligence-collection angle — detectable broadband RF discharge signatures of adversary devices

> As mentioned above, ultracapacitors could be important in the development of ultrawideband energy weapons. Previously, this market has been dominated by bulk acoustic semiconductor switch (BASS) devices, but by stringing several capacitors together in parallel, a more formidable discharge can be created. Because ultracapacitors have very high discharge rates, they will emit a distinctive broadband RF signature when discharged. A modest wideband receiver may be able to detect this discharge; its proximity will depend upon the construction of the target device.
